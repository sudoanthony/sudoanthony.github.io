About

Hello everyone. My name is Anthony Sciuto.

Since I was a child I grew a love for computers through video games. I am also a hobbyist illustrator, animator, and game developer. As a professional, I spent seven and a half years in the U.S. Navy in the medical field as a Hospital Corpsman. I was lucky enough to serve as a devil doc alongside Marine units, and I found that the ability to keep a cool head while working in time-critical, life-saving situations had a direct correlation to my pursuit of cybersecurity.

I now spend my time working in Japan, studying Japanese, and studying cybersecurity, mainly penetration testing. That ranges from web applications, wifi, and cloud, to more recently mobile penetration testing.

A common trend in all of the jobs I've worked and excelled in was the ability to help others. Whether it was the teaching I did in the Navy as a TCCC/CLS instructor or the work I did at hotels, I love helping others and find communication an invaluable skill in any field. Working in the medical field gave me countless repetitions of customer interactions, from civilian babies to seniors to military personnel. At the end of the day, the services we provide to others are fundamentally about helping them. If we remember that when providing a service, the quality and connection we give a client is something that can't really be expressed in a purely monetary transaction. Anyone can spam boxes and study, but we also have to remember that human skills are becoming more and more valuable as AI becomes a bigger part of everyone's lives.

Speaking of AI. When it comes to AI and art, I do not use AI whatsoever in the process or development of my art. While AI is a tool that I enjoy and continue to master, as an artist I want to preserve the creativity and skill that I am able to express as it is. AI as a tool should be used to level up and express our own skills, not to replace them. I am not saying I can't see any valuable use of AI in art, but there are a lot of people using AI for art and calling themselves an artist. That said, I do understand that in the professional world, if you're not maximizing the tools given to you then you're putting yourself at a disadvantage against those who are.

I've also noticed that one reason many people underutilize AI in their work is a lack of creativity and planning when prompting it. I truly believe that having creative hobbies, or a mind for solving problems, is essential to using AI effectively. You see many people even asking AI itself how to use AI effectively. That isn't bad, but we have to remember that we are the one prompting it, and if you can't do that part well, you won't have much better luck.

The use of AI can be compared to steroids. I love the gym, but I also love my health, and if there were a medicine that could give steroid-like gains without the drawbacks, I'm pretty sure everyone would take it, myself included. An untrained person on steroids who trains ineffectively can still see dramatic gains compared to a natural lifter. But the true difference shows when a professional who understands dieting, programming, and technique takes them. The difference is monumental. Steroids aren't a perfect analogy for AI, but I think it's a pretty good one. In the hands of a novice, AI can produce results sometimes even faster than a professional, but no company wants only that. People who actually use AI well understand how it works and understand the work required to produce a given result. Companies want professionals who understand their trade, understand how AI works, and use it in tandem with their existing knowledge and skills. That is where the value is, and that is still a person in demand.

I do like the personal touch though. People read tons of resumes and GitHubs every day, and the 100% professional angle has to get boring, no?
